Early is not the same as informal

Early dispute resolution is often treated as the absence of process. In reality, it is a process of its own, and perhaps the least forgiving one.

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · October 19, 2026 · 3 min read

If you ask me which question I hear most often from HR leaders, it is not about investigations. It is this: can we resolve this early on informally?

Most of the time, it is the right instinct.

A large share of workplace conflict does not need a committee, a notice or a finding. It needs a well-timed conversation before positions harden and before people start telling the story to colleagues instead of to each other. I trained as a mediator because I have seen, across more than three hundred investigations, how many matters did not need to become investigations at all.

What many organisations call 'informal' is not the absence of process. It is a process of its own. The difficulty is that because it appears lighter and faster than an investigation, organisations often assume it requires less discipline. In practice, it may require more.

In most organisations I work with, early resolution is understood as the opposite of process. A manager has a conversation. HR brings two people together. Everyone agrees to move forward. Little or nothing is recorded because the point was to keep the matter informal.

Most of the time, that is the end of it. But some matters return.

Six months later, the same concern reappears as a formal complaint, and the earlier attempt to resolve it becomes part of the dispute. One person recalls being encouraged to "let it go". Another says the issue was settled. The manager remembers a constructive conversation.

Yet there is no clear record of what was raised, what was agreed, what options were discussed, or whether everyone genuinely felt able to participate. At that point, the early resolution has not simply failed. It has become evidence, and the organisation has none to refer back to. That is why I think early resolution deserves to be treated with more care.

Not because it should become legalistic, slow or bureaucratic. Quite the opposite. Its value lies in speed, flexibility and pragmatism. But those qualities do not eliminate the need for judgement. They make judgement more important. In my experience, three points of failure appear repeatedly.

The first is route selection.

Not every workplace concern belongs in an early resolution process. Some issues can be addressed through conversation, clarification or facilitated discussion. Others require a more structured response from the outset.

The challenge is that organisations often make this decision quickly and intuitively. The choice may be right, but the reasoning behind it is rarely articulated. When the matter later escalates, people are left trying to reconstruct why an informal route was chosen in the first place.

The second is consent.

Agreement only means something when people feel genuinely free to choose.

That sounds obvious, but workplace relationships are rarely equal. Differences in seniority, reporting lines, influence and experience can all affect whether someone feels able to decline an informal process. What appears to be a mutual agreement from the organisation's perspective may not have felt voluntary to everyone involved.

The issue is not whether people said yes. It is whether they felt able to say no.

The third is role.

The person facilitating an informal resolution often becomes the person advising on, assessing or investigating the same matter later. Organisations usually see this as efficient. Parties often see it differently.

Information shared in an attempt to resolve a conflict carries a different character from information gathered as evidence. Once those roles begin to blur, questions about neutrality and fairness tend to follow.

The problem may not be real bias. The problem is that confidence in the process can be lost long before anyone reaches a conclusion. None of this requires turning early resolution into a formal investigation. It simply requires enough structure for the process to explain itself later.

Why was this route chosen? Who agreed to it? Were alternatives discussed? What was resolved? What remained unresolved?

A short record is often enough. The objective is not to create paperwork. It is to ensure the process can explain itself later. Organisations often treat informal resolution as the soft option. In reality, it asks people to make difficult judgments quickly, with limited information and very little room to revisit the decision later.

That is why it deserves more discipline, not less. Early resolution is not the absence of process. It is a process of its own. And because it leaves so little behind, it may be the process with the least room for error.
