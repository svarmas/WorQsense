Trained to manage, asked to investigate

Indian companies hand grievance investigations to the people they trust most, their own HR and never train them for the one part that is a craft entirely of its own. The law has just made that gap expensive.

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · September 2, 2026 · 4 min read

If you ask me where a grievance process most often goes wrong in India, it is not at the finding. It is at the very beginning, in the quiet decision about who will handle the inquiry.

In most organisations, the answer is internal HR. It is the natural choice: they are present, they are trusted, they know the people and the place. And then we ask them to do something no part of their training prepared them for, to investigate.

This is not a criticism of HR. HR professionals are skilled, often exceptionally so, at the work they were actually trained to do: managing people, easing tension, holding an organisation together through friction that never reaches a formal process. But investigating a grievance is a different craft with a different purpose. Managing a situation is about restoring a working relationship; investigating one is about establishing what happened, fairly, in a way a stranger can later follow. The instincts that make someone good at the first can quietly work against them in the second - the urge to smooth, to reassure, to resolve, when the task calls for gathering evidence, questioning without leading, testing an account against what else is known, and leaving behind a record that holds its shape under pressure.

None of that arrives with an HR qualification. It has to be taught. And for years it did not need to be, because grievance handling in India was largely informal a conversation, a note to file, a matter closed quietly. The absence of training did not show, because nothing tested it.

That has changed. Under the new labour codes, a grievance redressal committee (#GRC) is now mandatory for establishments with twenty or more workers, with a thirty-day clock and a defined path that runs from the committee to conciliation and, if unresolved, to an industrial tribunal. Grievance handling is no longer a private conversation that ends when tempers cool. It is a structured, time-bound, appealable process and the file it produces may one day be read cold by someone who was not in the room and owes the organisation no benefit of the doubt.

That is where an untrained inquiry comes apart. Not because the person handling it lacked judgment, but because the record cannot carry the judgment without them standing next to it. Evidence gathered without method. An interview that led the witness to the answer. A step taken but never documented, a reason held in someone's head and never written down. Each is the kind of gap a sound decision can still contain and the first thing a tribunal, an appeal, or a lawyer reading the file will find. There is a quieter cost, too: employees who watch a process handled without rigour and decide, next time, that it is not worth raising anything at all.

The instinct at this point is to send the hard matters out, to bring in an external investigator. For the most serious and complex cases, that is exactly right, and it is work I do. But no organisation can outsource every grievance, nor should it want to. The volume is too high, the everyday matters too many, and the person who first receives a concern will almost always be internal. Internal capability is not a fallback to external expertise; it is the foundation beneath it. The question is not whether HR will investigate. They already do. The question is whether they have been equipped to.

And this is the part worth sitting with: equipping them is the most closable gap a company has. It needs no change in the law, no new committee, no structural reform. It needs the people already doing the work trained in the methods that make an inquiry defensible — how to plan and conduct an interview, how to test an account without coercing it, how to build a record that explains itself. That is a learnable discipline, not an innate talent, and far cheaper to build than the cost of a single finding that does not survive.

This is the gap WORQsense builds; training internal HR and committees in investigative interviewing and defensible inquiry, the PEACE method paired with the systems that keep it consistent. For a business operating under real exposure, this is not optional. It is the difference between an inquiry that endures and one that fails quietly when pressure arrives.

We already ask HR to investigate. The only real decision left is whether to train them for it.
