Pressure is not rigour

In India, most people who conduct workplace‑investigation interviews were never trained to. Left to instinct, questioning becomes interrogation and interrogation produces the one thing an inquiry cannot afford: an account you can no longer trust. There has been a better method for thirty years, used across much of the world and only now being brought into India’s workplace investigations.

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · August 13, 2026 · 6 min read

If you ask me what’s the most consequential part of a workplace investigation, without a blink I would say interviewing. Almost every workplace investigation is decided in the interview room. Not in the complaint that opens it, not in the report that closes it, but in the hours between, where an investigator sits across from a person and tries to turn an account into evidence. It is also, in most Indian organisations, the part no one was trained to do.

In India, the people who end up conducting these interviews are rarely investigators by training. They are HR leaders or business partners, managers, heads of functions; competent, senior, trusted, and almost never taught how to question a person whose account matters. There is no professional standard the role is held to, and no accreditation it requires; nothing in the credential that put them in the room prepared them for the room. Elsewhere, the gap was taken seriously enough to build a discipline around it. In India, it has been left to instinct.

And instinct, untrained, defaults to one thing: confrontation. The confrontation instinct feels like rigour. Press the person. Catch the inconsistency. Circle back to the contradiction and watch them squirm. Read hesitation as evasion and push on it. It looks like thoroughness, and it is what most of us picture when we picture an investigation, because it is what we have been shown one looks like, in every interrogation scene we have ever watched.

It is also the most reliable way to manufacture evidence that cannot be trusted. Pressure does not produce truth. It produces whatever ends the pressure; the anxious admitting to things that did not happen, the guilty and the innocent alike falling silent, accounts quietly bent toward whatever the questioner has made clear they want to hear. At the moment it feels most like rigour, the interview stops gathering information and starts contaminating it. And a contaminated account does not announce itself. It sits in the file looking exactly like evidence, until someone with no stake in the outcome picks it apart.

The strange part is that the profession already knows this. Its own practice guidance in India says, in as many words, that questioning should be conducted as an interview and not an interrogation, that the person should be put at ease, that the investigator should not walk in expecting a confession, that pressure has no place in it. The principle is settled. What is missing is the thing that turns a principle into a practice: a method, learned and repeatable, rather than a good intention improvised under stress.

That method exists, it is called PEACE, and it was built in England and Wales in the early 1990s, after a run of wrongful convictions founded on coerced confessions forced a reckoning, the recognition that interrogation designed to extract admissions was producing unreliable evidence and unsafe outcomes. The response was to stop interrogating and start interviewing: prepare before entering the room, build rapport and explain the process, let the person give their whole account before testing any of it, then challenge that account against the evidence rather than against their nerves. It has since become the professional standard for investigative interviewing across the United Kingdom, New Zealand and Australia; used for everything from serious crime to corporate misconduct; not because it is gentler, but because it is more accurate. An account given freely and then tested is worth more than an account extracted and then doubted.

This is not a fashion that is passing. It is the direction the whole field is moving. In 2021 an international committee of experts published the Méndez Principles, named for Juan Méndez, the former United Nations Special Rapporteur on Torture who first called for them, which reframed non‑coercive, rapport‑based interviewing not merely as good tradecraft but as a standard grounded in human rights and in the science of how memory and disclosure actually work. Since then the approach has moved from principle into practice: the United Nations issued a manual built on it in 2024, dedicated Méndez Centres have been set up to train it, and a cross‑border implementation network (#ImpleMéndez) now spans dozens of countries working to embed it. The world’s investigative bodies are converging on precisely the discipline most workplace inquiries have never been given.

And the cost of getting this wrong in India is rising, fast. The country’s new labour codes, notified in November 2025, build something Indian workplaces have never really had: a formal grievance machinery with a clock and an exit. Establishments with twenty or more workers now sit inside a system where an internal dispute can travel, in a matter of weeks, from a redressal committee to conciliation to an industrial tribunal. At the same time the misconduct itself is multiplying, hybrid and cross‑border working, dual employment, conduct that plays out across time zones, to the point that even the large consultancies have begun telling HR to stop administering and start investigating. More inquiries, higher stakes, and a far shorter path out of the building to an outside forum.

For anyone who conducts an investigation, the point is not ideology. It is defensibility. An account obtained under pressure is not only unkind; it is fragile. Whatever forum eventually reads the file cold, a disciplinary appeal, an industrial tribunal, a civil court, a regulator, a finding built on a person who “eventually admitted it” after an hour of pressure, or one led through their own account by someone who had already decided, is a finding waiting to be undone. A disciplined interview produces two things confrontation never can: a fuller, cleaner account, and a record that shows the account was obtained fairly. The second is very often what survives when the first is challenged.

And this is what makes it the highest‑leverage change available to anyone who investigates. It requires no new rule, no new law, no structural reform. The method already exists. The rest of the investigative world already uses it. The science has already validated it. What stands between most organisations and a defensible interview is not a gap in the law. It is a gap in training and that is the most closable gap there is.

And the method need not stay a matter of individual skill. In the jurisdictions that took interviewing seriously, it was carried by systems, platforms that hold the plan, the process and the record across an organisation, so that quality can be supervised, outcomes evaluated, and consistency proven rather than hoped for. That is how a good interview stops depending on who happens to be in the room.

None of this has to be built from scratch. This is the discipline Interview Management Solutions (IMS) has built and proven internationally, and it is the one WORQsense now brings to Indian organisations, the PEACE method paired with the systems that make it defensible at scale. For a business carrying real exposure, that is not a theoretical nicety. It is the difference between an inquiry that holds under scrutiny and one that quietly falls apart.

The investigation is only ever as good as the interview. In India, the method and the means to run it well, now exist. The interview no longer has to be a guess.
