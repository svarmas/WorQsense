Ten questions your PoSH audit won't ask

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · July 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Before the essay, one question.

No, not "is your organisation PoSH compliant?" - you have answered that one a dozen times this year. This one: if your last inquiry were challenged in court tomorrow, would the case file survive?

Notice what just happened as you read that. "Compliant" you answered instantly. This one, you're not sure. Most leaders aren't - not because their teams are careless, but because nobody has ever examined the inquiry itself. Plenty of people will check whether your PoSH system exists. Almost nobody tests whether it would survive.

That’s the work I do, and I have distilled its first pass into an instrument: the PoSH Defensibility Audit. Ten questions, two pages, drawn from what courts actually examined in 2025. And if you would rather skip the audit and get a real answer: bring me one closed case file. Within a week, you will know exactly where it would have held and where it would have failed under challenge - question by question, with reasons, in a finding you could hand to your board or your external counsel unedited. One file, fixed scope, fixed fee. Write "one case file" in the subject line and I will take it from there.

Now, the essay - which explains why these ten are the questions that decide cases, and why the coming audits won't ask them.

Last edition, I argued that India's harassment framework is a seatbelt law: it measures the quality of our response to harm, almost never the conditions that produce it. Three hundred of you subscribed in the two weeks since. And the piece left the obvious question hanging: fine, but the seatbelt inspection is coming anyway. The National Commission for Women (NCW) now wants annual audits - conducted by establishments themselves, reported to District Authorities, with non-conduct itself treated as non-compliance. And note the scope the advisory actually sets: not just whether committees exist, but how they function and what happened to complaints. So what should such an audit examine?

There is a wave of audit-readiness advice going around right now, and most of it is right as far as it goes. Reconstitute your committee. Review your policy. File your annual reports. Register on SHe-Box. Keep your training records, brief your leadership, mind your disclosures. That checklist deserves the attention it's getting, and audit templates and advisories from PoSH consultants are flying.

But an audit of artefacts tells you whether the system exists. It cannot tell you whether the system works. For that, you have to open the case files.

Recent rulings, sharpened through 2025, have made this distinction unmissable. Findings were quashed and employment actions struck down in organisations that had every artefact in place. The policy existed. The committee was constituted. The training happened. What failed was the inquiry itself: complaints classified wrongly at intake, complaints lost to the clock because limitation was never assessed, interviews conducted without plans, records that showed answers but not questions, findings unmoored from the standard of proof, complainants who suffered for coming forward while the matter was pending. The belt was fitted. It was even worn. But everyone wrote their own instruction manual.

So here is what I believe a PoSH audit must actually test, beyond the artefacts.

Classification at intake. Was each complaint assessed and routed deliberately: PoSH, grievance, or misconduct? The Kerala High Court held in 2025 (Abraham Mathai v. State of Kerala, 2025:KER:57427) that hostility, unfair treatment, or termination without an unwelcome sexual element belongs to labour law, not PoSH - and in the same matter, the committee's report was set aside because witness statements were taken over the telephone, with no chance to challenge them. An audit should find a documented classification decision at the top of every file.

The interview record. Not whether interviews happened, but whether they were planned before they happened, opened properly, and documented so the record shows what was asked, not just what was answered. A record that cannot show the question cannot defend the answer. This is where most inquiries quietly fail, and no artefact checklist will ever surface it.

The reasoning in the findings. Was the balance of probabilities applied and stated? Were credibility assessments written down as reasons tied to evidence, rather than impressions of demeanour? Was conduct assessed on its impact, using the reasonable-woman standard the Madras High Court expressly adopted in 2025 (HCL Technologies v. N. Parsarathy, 2025:MHC:202), rather than on the respondent's stated intent? As that court put it, CCTV cannot prove or disprove intention - "all that can be understood is how it was felt by the recipients." Were parties given the chance to respond to findings before the report was finalised?

The complainant's experience of the process. Did her employment, pay, role, and dignity survive the inquiry intact? The Delhi High Court, in the Sahitya Akademi matter (2021:DHC:3343), quashed a termination issued while the complaint was pending, holding the employer's duty is to assist the aggrieved woman, not oppose her. An audit that doesn't examine what happened to the person who complained has missed the point of the Act.

What changed afterwards. If a complaint was upheld and nothing about the system changed, the organisation bought a report, not a resolution. The next complaint is already forming in the same conditions. A serious audit asks: what did this case teach you, and where is the evidence that you learned it? This is where the seatbelt inspection, done honestly, starts pointing at the road.

None of this shows up in a documentation trail, because none of it is documentation. It's capability. Committees conduct defensible inquiries when their members have been trained to interview, to weigh evidence, and to write findings that survive challenge. That training is specialised work, and the NCW advisory names it: specialised training for committee members so that inquiries are fair, sensitive, and legally sound. It is, quite literally, the manual.

The organisations that will pass the coming audits comfortably are not the ones with the neatest binders. They are the ones whose case files defend themselves.

If you have read this far, you already know which of these checks would make your organisation flinch. That's the one to look at - before an auditor, a tribunal, or the next complaint looks at it for you.
