What I heard in India: the questions organisations are asking about workplace complaints now

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · October 1, 2026 · 3 min read

I have just come back from several weeks in India: many meetings, a lot of travel, and more conversations than I could have planned. I spoke with HR heads, IC members, in-house and external counsel, and compliance leaders across sectors.

I went in expecting to talk about PoSH. What I heard was bigger than that. The Supreme Court decision in Aureliano Fernandes v State of Goa has clearly had an impact. It brought much more attention to whether organisations were actually meeting their PoSH obligations, rather than simply having a policy and an IC on paper.

But what was interesting was where the conversations have gone since. The question I kept hearing wasn't “Are we compliant?” It was “Will our process hold up?”

Here are five things that stayed with me.

1. Compliance is now assumed. What people want to know is whether it will hold up.

Most organisations I met have the basics in place: an IC, a policy, annual training, a filed report. Nobody was really asking how to set up an IC anymore. They wanted to know what happens when a finding is challenged. Can the IC show how it reached its conclusion, not just what it concluded? Can someone outside the organisation follow the reasoning? That's a different question. And the answer is method, not paperwork.

2. IC members are carrying more than they were trained for.

Again and again, I heard some version of: “Our members are committed, but they're not investigators.” They are being asked to plan interviews, assess credibility, weigh conflicting accounts and write reasoned findings, usually alongside their full-time jobs.

The issue isn't goodwill. It's that the role involves a quasi-judicial function, and people are often stepping into it after relatively limited training.

3. Employee Relations is becoming a function in its own right.

Several organisations told me they are seriously considering setting up a dedicated Employee Relations (ER) team. The reason was pretty consistent: misconduct and harassment complaints are increasing, and generalist HR teams are not necessarily trained to handle them.

For some, complaints have stopped being occasional. They're now part of the daily workload. At that volume, HR can't treat each complaint as a side task and still run every matter well.

Taking evidence, keeping roles separate, managing conflicts of interest and writing a defensible report are specialist skills. When those tasks sit with whoever happens to be available, you get inconsistency. And inconsistent processes are the ones that struggle when challenged.

An ER function is only as good as the capability built into it. Hiring people into ER roles is the easy part. Giving them a consistent method and the training to apply it is what makes the difference.

4. The Industrial Relations Code is opening a second front.

With organisations now looking at their Grievance Redressal Committee obligations, I heard another concern: “Are we about to build a second IC-like structure with the same capability gaps?”

It's a fair question. The GRC and the IC deal with different problems, but they share one need: a fair, documented and defensible process for hearing grievances and reaching conclusions. Organisations that build that capability once, properly, will be better placed for both.

5. Not everything needs a finding.

This one surprised me most. Several leaders talked about complaints that weren't really misconduct. They were broken working relationships, poor communication, or a manager who had lost the team's trust. A formal process gave those complaints a finding. But it didn't necessarily fix anything.

There is growing interest in knowing which matters need investigation and which need a structured conversation. Making that call well at the start, triage, may be the most underrated skill in this field.

I came away thinking that the conversation around workplace complaints in India is changing. Compliance still matters. But increasingly, that's the starting point.

The harder question is whether the people running these processes have the method, judgement and capability to do them well.
