The Seatbelt and the Road

India has spent thirteen years perfecting how it responds to harassment. It has spent almost none on the conditions that produce it. A new National Commission for Women (NCW) audit advisory is about to make that imbalance visible.

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · July 15, 2026 · 7 min read

Last month the National Commission for Women (NCW) issued an advisory that most compliance teams read as a warning and I read as a confession.

On 19 June 2026, the NCW asked every state and union territory to make annual prevention of sexual harassment at workplace (PoSH) audits mandatory for establishments with ten or more employees. The audit checks whether the Internal Committee is properly constituted, whether complaints are being handled, whether confidentiality holds, whether the mandatory disclosures exist, whether Sexual Harassment Electronic Box (SHe-Box) is being used. Non-conduct of the audit is itself treated as non-compliance, exposed to penalty.

It is a sensible advisory. It will catch the organisations that have a committee on paper and nothing behind it and there are more of those than anyone in a boardroom would like to admit. I am not against it. I want to be careful about that, because what I am about to say will sound like criticism and it isn't.

The advisory is necessary. It is also a very precise photograph of what India built instead of prevention.

A seatbelt law

Think about what a seatbelt does.

It does nothing until the crash. It changes no driver's behaviour, redesigns no intersection, removes no blind corner. It sits, unused, until the moment of impact and then it reduces the harm of a collision that has already happened.

Seatbelt laws are good law. They save lives. No serious person argues against them. But no country has ever driven its road deaths down with seatbelts alone. The countries that made real progress did something else, something slower and less visible: they engineered the roads. Sightlines. Speed by design. Junction geometry. Lighting. They changed the conditions under which crashes occur, so that fewer occur at all.

The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention Prohibition and Redressal) Act 2013 (the Act) is a seatbelt law.

It activates after the complaint. Its entire machinery, the internal committee, the inquiry, the timeline, the finding, the report; is a response to an impact that has already happened. Someone has already been harmed. The Act does not ask why the road bent the way it did. It asks whether the seatbelt was fastened correctly when the car hit the wall.

For thirteen years, that is what we have measured. Trainings conducted. Committees constituted. Complaints disposed. Reports filed. Every one of those numbers describes the quality of our response to harm. Not one of them describes the conditions that produced it.

The NCW advisory audit measures the same thing, one level up. It is an inspection of the response machinery. A good inspection but of the ambulance, not the road.

Activity is not safety

Here is the quiet substitution at the centre of the Indian PoSH system: we began measuring activity and called it safety.

A company with a full training calendar, a properly constituted committee, timely disposals and a clean audit trail can produce all of that while being an unsafe place to work. The metrics will glow. The audit will pass. And the conditions that make people afraid to speak; proximity to power, a first receiver who flinches, a leader whose name lowers the temperature in a room; sit entirely outside the frame, because none of them generate a number.

I have written before about how much of the most important evidence in an investigation was never in the case file. The same is true of the system as a whole. The things that determine whether a workplace is actually safe are precisely the things a compliance audit cannot see, because an audit is designed to inspect what happened after a complaint, not the pressure that decided whether a complaint was ever made.

We privatised the adjudication - handed quasi-judicial fact-finding to internal committees, without building the capacity to do it well, and then we built a compliance apparatus to check that the machinery exists. What we never built was the road engineering: any system that reads the conditions before the crash.

This is not a failure of any one committee or any one HR team. Most of the internal committees I work with are staffed by conscientious people doing careful work with almost no support. It is a design choice, made at the level of the framework itself. We chose the seatbelt. We have been surprised, ever since, that the crashes keep coming.

What a road engineer does differently

I did not learn this in New Zealand. Long before I moved here, I was already telling my Indian clients the same thing: treat harassment as a health and safety problem, not only a legal one. Build for the conditions, not just the complaint. Some of them heard it as a nice idea and filed it next to the training calendar. It was, at the time, a practitioner's instinct without a system behind it, I was describing a road nobody had been asked to build.

Then I moved, and found a legal system that had already built it. That is the part worth sitting with. The insight was not New Zealand's gift to me. New Zealand was the proof that the thing I had been arguing in Indian boardrooms was not idealism. It was engineering, and somewhere it had already been done.

New Zealand does not have a PoSH Act. It has the Health and Safety at Work Act (HSWA), and under it, harassment, sexual harassment, bullying, the whole family of interpersonal harm, is treated as a workplace hazard. Not a legal event that triggers a committee. A hazard. The same word the law uses for a chemical spill or an unguarded machine.

That single reclassification changes everything downstream.

A hazard has to be identified before it causes harm. It has to be assessed, monitored, and so far as reasonably practicable, eliminated or minimised. The duty is proactive and it is continuous; it does not wait for someone to be injured. And it does not sit with a committee convened after the fact. It sits, personally and not capable of being delegated, with the people who govern the organisation. Directors. The board. Officers who can be held to account not for how well they responded to harm, but for whether they took reasonable steps to prevent it.

That is road engineering. Leading indicators instead of lagging ones. Conditions instead of incidents. Accountability that attaches before the crash, not after.

I am not arguing India should import the HSWA. The legal architecture is different, the enforcement landscape is different, and a straight transplant would fail. I am arguing that the New Zealand model shows something the Indian debate has almost entirely lost sight of: that it is possible to build a system that looks at the road, and that the absence of one is a choice, not an inevitability.

Necessary, never sufficient

So here is what I think the NCW advisory actually is.

It is the system reaching the limit of what a seatbelt law can do, and responding by tightening the seatbelt. Audit the response. Formalise the inspection. Penalise the organisations whose machinery is fake. All good. All necessary.

And all of it still measured in the wrong place, after the impact, on the quality of the belt, never on the shape of the road.

An audit can tell you whether your committee is constituted correctly. It cannot tell you whether anyone believes it is safe to walk into that committee's room. It can confirm your disclosures are posted. It cannot tell you what your best people have already decided not to report. It measures the machinery of response and calls the reading a measure of safety, and the two are not the same thing, they have never been the same thing.

The advisory is being received, in most rooms I watch, as though it were sufficient. Do the audit, and you are safe. That is the seatbelt mistake, restated at the level of policy.

The maturing of PoSH obligations, the arrival of Grievance Redressal Committees under the Industrial Relations Code, the audit mandate now landing on top of both, India is at an inflection point. It could use this moment to build more inspection of the same machinery. Or it could ask the question the whole framework has managed to avoid for thirteen years.

Not: did the seatbelt work?

But: who is looking at the road?

Note: This is the first in a series on why India's Sexual Harassment at Workplace (Prevention Prohibition and Redressal) framework measures its response to harm far more carefully than the conditions that produce it and what a system built the other way around would look like.
