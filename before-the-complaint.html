Before the Complaint

What “All Reasonable Steps” Really Means

By Sreevidya Varma, AWI-CH · November 21, 2026 · 3 min read

If you ask me what changed in the United Kingdom (UK) this year, I would put it in one sentence. Employers are no longer judged only by how they handle harassment complaints. They are increasingly judged by what they did before there was a complaint to handle. That sounds like a subtle change. It is not.

This edition takes a brief look at the current legal obligations on employers in three jurisdictions: the UK, India and New Zealand

The UK's question has changed

In 2024, employers became subject to a duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment. In October 2026, that became a duty to take all reasonable steps. The same reforms restored employer liability for harassment by third parties such as customers, clients and contractors unless the employer can show it took all reasonable steps to prevent it. The details of that standard are still developing, but the direction is clear.

The question is no longer:

"What did you do when the complaint arrived?"

It is increasingly:

"What were you doing before it arrived?"

What this means for investigations

First, the complaint process has become evidence. How previous concerns were received, investigated and acted upon may now form part of the answer to whether an organisation took all reasonable steps. An investigation that was delayed, poorly scoped or never acted upon may say as much about prevention as it does about the complaint itself.

Second, third-party matters become organisational questions. Employers cannot usually discipline customers or clients. What can always be examined is the organisation's own conduct: what it knew, what it did and what options were available to it.

Third, earlier signals matter more. Informal reports, near-misses and patterns of behaviour become important. A concern resolved informally and left undocumented may later appear less like successful resolution and more like missing evidence.

Fourth, independence deserves attention earlier. Where the same team designed the prevention measures and now investigates whether they worked, questions of independence are difficult to avoid.

India asks a different question

India has had express employer obligations for much longer through the PoSH Act. That framework has a real strength: employers know exactly what is required of them. But it asks a different question. The focus is largely on whether prescribed duties were carried out rather than whether prevention measures were adequate for a particular workplace or demonstrably effective.

Compared with the UK's evolving framework:

  • Training must occur, but the law does not ask whether it changed behaviour.

  • There is no explicit requirement to assess where risks are most likely to arise.

  • Third-party conduct is addressed indirectly rather than through direct employer liability.

The contrast is not one of stronger or weaker protection. It is a difference in what evidence matters.

New Zealand asks a broader question

New Zealand takes a different approach again. Rather than relying on a single prevention duty, it addresses workplace harassment through employment law, human rights law and health and safety law. That combination is interesting because each framework looks at the issue differently.

Employment law asks what happened to the worker. Human rights law asks whether equality and dignity were respected. Health and safety law asks what could have been done to prevent harm.

There is much to admire in that model. It recognises that workplace harassment is not simply misconduct between individuals. It is also a question of organisational culture, psychological safety and human dignity.

In that sense, New Zealand may already possess many of the tools associated with modern prevention. From April 2027, health and safety law will ask businesses to prioritise critical risks. That makes this a useful moment for organisations to decide, deliberately, where psychosocial risks, including harassment, sit in their own prevention planning, before a complaint is ever made.

The signal

The UK, India and New Zealand arrive at prevention from different directions. The UK frames it increasingly as a legal duty. India frames it through specific statutory obligations. New Zealand approaches it through employment, human rights and health and safety frameworks. But each is ultimately grappling with the same question:

What did the organisation do before the complaint, and can it show it?

In my work with Indian companies, almost every investigation report has ended with recommendations to employer to implement better preventive strategies to avoid similar future incidents. For investigators, that matters because the investigation is no longer simply about establishing what happened. Increasingly, it also examines whether the organisation's prevention measures were adequate and where they fell short.
